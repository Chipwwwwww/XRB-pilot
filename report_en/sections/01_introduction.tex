\section{The scientific question}\label{sec:question}

An accreting compact object in an X-ray binary is either a neutron star (NS) or a stellar-mass black hole (BH). The
only direct way to tell them apart is a dynamical mass (from the companion's radial-velocity curve) above the maximum NS
mass, or direct evidence of a solid surface (thermonuclear type-I X-ray bursts) or of a magnetosphere anchored to it
(coherent pulsations). Most X-ray binaries -- in particular new transients -- have neither when they are discovered. The
project asks whether a \emph{single X-ray observation} carries enough information to say which kind of compact object it
contains, \emph{for a source that was never seen during training}.

Physical reasons to expect some information exist. In soft (thermal, disc-dominated) states, an NS has a boundary layer
or surface emission in addition to the disc, while a BH shows a cooler disc plus a power-law tail, so their count-space
colours occupy different regions \citep[e.g.][]{DoneGierlinski2003}. In hard (Comptonisation-dominated) states the spectra
are similar, but the variability may differ: the characteristic time-scales of the aperiodic variability are expected to
scale with the size of the inner flow, hence with mass, and NSs show high-frequency noise above $\sim$500\,Hz that BHs lack
\citep{SunyaevRevnivtsev2000}. The variability amplitude also tracks the state \citep{RemillardMcClintock2006,MunozDarias2011}.
Whether these differences survive a strict test on unseen sources was the open question.

\section{Why the test must be on unseen sources}\label{sec:unseen}

Repeated observations of one source are strongly correlated: the same distance, absorption column, inclination,
companion, outburst history and calibration epoch shape all of them. A classifier evaluated on a random split of
observations can score well simply by recognising the source of an observation -- through its brightness, its absorption
or its particular track in a colour diagram -- without having learned anything that transfers to a new object. The
relevant population for the question is the population of \emph{sources}; the effective sample size is the number of
sources (here 7--16 BHs), not the number of observations (hundreds to thousands). Every primary result in this report is
therefore obtained with leave-one-source-out (LOSO) evaluation, all choices that depend on data are made inside the
training sources (\cref{sec:nested}), and uncertainty is quantified by resampling sources (\cref{sec:bootstrap}).

\section{Data leakage and how large it is here}\label{sec:leakage}

\emph{Leakage} is any path through which information about the test units enters training. In this problem the main path
is source identity: if observations of the same source are in both training and test sets, the test score measures
re-identification. The project quantified it directly: the same representations and models were evaluated once with LOSO
and once with a stratified 5-fold split over observations (\cref{fig:leakage}). The leaky split overstates balanced
accuracy by $+0.04$ to $+0.20$ (largest for the Random Forest on the intensity-keeping representation A, $+0.16$ in v2 and
$+0.20$ in v1). Published machine-learning classifications of RXTE spectra used either LOSO with sigma-clipped source
accuracies \citep[87\%;][]{Pattnaik2021} or random 80:20 observation splits \citep[90--94\%;][]{Garg2026}; the latter cannot
answer the question about new sources. A MAXI study \citep{deBeurs2022} used LOSO but labelled seven BH candidates as BHs; it is
reproduced and re-examined in \cref{ch:phase4}.

\section{Labels}\label{sec:intro-labels}

Only sources whose nature is established independently of their X-ray spectra and variability are used as labels:
dynamically confirmed BHs (BlackCAT, \citealt{CorralSantana2016}; \citealt{CasaresJonker2014}; from v7 the 25-system list of
\citealt{Marcel2026}) and NSs with type-I bursts \citep{Galloway2008,Galloway2020} or accretion-powered pulsations
\citep{PatrunoWatts2021}. BH \emph{candidates} are never used as labels (they appear only as prediction targets and in a
label-sensitivity analysis, v3c). This keeps the labels honest but limits the sample: across all instruments only 16
dynamically confirmed BHs have usable RXTE or NICER data, and only 7 of them are in the main RXTE sample.

\section{Design principles and their limits}\label{sec:principles}

The project ran as 13 analysis versions (v1--v13) plus a wrap-up (v14), between 2026-09-29 and 2026-10-06, each with a
written pre-registration committed before its data were downloaded or its models trained, and with a decision log
(\file{reports/decision\_log.md}) recording every change and whether it was made before or after seeing results. The
rules (fixed from v4 on) are: LOSO; source score = mean OOF score, threshold 0.5; 2000 class-stratified source bootstrap
replicates (seed 42); a difference is \emph{detected} only if its 95\% interval excludes 0 (or $p<0.05$ for permutation
tests); one (or very few) pre-declared primary comparison per sub-analysis; every selection nested inside the training
sources.

\begin{cautionbox}{Five facts that qualify every conclusion in this report}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Every pre-registration was written after the results of all earlier versions had been seen.} The
pre-registrations prevent analysis choices from adapting to the data of their own version, but the hypotheses of later
versions were suggested by earlier results (e.g.\ the hard-state timing hypothesis of v8 came from v5's descriptive
results). They were approved by the analyst on the user's instruction, not by an external reviewer.
\item \textbf{The v10 pooled tests are overall evidence on all available data, not an independent confirmation}: the
hypotheses and the pooling scheme were formulated after v5--v9.
\item \textbf{v11--v13 are new observations of the same sources}: the prospective confirmation (v13) is at the
observation level; nothing has yet been confirmed on new sources.
\item \textbf{The Random Forest was chosen as primary post hoc in v12} (after seeing that its timing gain was stable in
v9--v11 while LR's was not); \textbf{in v13 it was chosen before the data were downloaded.}
\item \textbf{Only 16 dynamically confirmed BH sources} have usable RXTE/NICER data; per instrument the hard-like BH
sources number 5--9. A single BH source moves a source-level balanced accuracy by $\approx0.07$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{cautionbox}

\section{Reading guide}\label{sec:guide}

\Cref{ch:features,ch:datamodel,ch:methods,ch:evaluation} define all inputs, data structures, models and statistical
procedures once. The six phase chapters then report the versions in groups of related analyses
(\cref{tab:phasemap}); each starts with a ``Phase at a glance'' box and follows the same ten sections. Each result carries
a tag: \Primary{} (the pre-declared primary comparison of its sub-analysis), \Secondary{} (pre-declared, not primary),
\Descriptive{} (pre-declared description without a decision rule), \Exploratory{} (pre-declared but downgraded, e.g.\ after
a failed implementation check) or \PostHoc{} (decided after seeing results). \Cref{ch:synthesis} brings the phases
together; \cref{ch:limitations,ch:future,ch:questions} discuss limitations, future work and questions for discussion.
